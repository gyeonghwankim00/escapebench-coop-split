Measuring the Collaboration Ability of LLM Agents in Escape Room
Team: Escape00

Problem definition and motivation. In multi-agent LLM systems, agents often see only part of the situation, so success also depends on whether what one agent observes reaches, and is used by, the agent that needs it. This information is often not given up front: agents must explore to find it and judge for themselves what their partner needs. We define collaboration ability as exchanging and integrating privately held information through dialogue to solve a problem that no single agent can solve alone, and measure it in a twoescape room situation: each agent observes and acts only in its own room, and the two interact only through dialogue (for information) and a hatch (for objects). Each clue lies in a different room from the lock it opens, and neither agent knows at the start which clues it holds or which the other needs. We call such a clue a cross-room clue and trace it through three stages: discovery, transmission, and uptake. A clue fails by non-discovery if its holder never observes it, by non-transmission if it is observed but never sent, or sent incorrectly or incompletely, and by non-uptake if it arrives correctly but is not used on its lock. Success rates alone cannot reveal the failing stage, yet the remedy depends on it.

Main idea. Beyond splitting rooms of the text escape-room benchmark Esca peBench (Qian et al., ACL 2025) in two, we rebuild each into two mutually invisible rooms whose locks depend on clues or objects in the other room, so the agents must interact to escape. The original solutions show which clue each lock needs, so failed clues get a stage from environment logs. We compare three conditions: Solo (one agent sees and acts in both rooms), Peer (two copies of one model, one per room), and Oracle (the evaluated agent with an LLM partner given the solution and both rooms' contents but, as in Peer, confined to its own room).

Contributions. (1) A clue-level analysis of an exploration task requiring collaboration that follows each cross-room clue from discovery through transmission to uptake and classifies every failure as non-discovery, non-transmission, or non-uptake. (2) A three-condition design that decomposes the cost of splitting information (Solo vs. Peer) into the cost of exchanging information by dialogue (Solo vs. Oracle) and that of the partner's imperfect information handling (Oracle vs. Peer).

Hypotheses. H1: Peer success is lower than Solo success. H2: The Solo–Peer gap grows with the number of cross-room clues, controlling for solution length. H3: Excluding non-discovery, Peer failures are mostly non-transmission, because agents must choose what to share without knowing the partner's needs; with an Oracle partner, failures shift to non-uptake. H4: In Peer, the share of non-transmission falls as model size grows, shifting failures toward non-uptake.

Literature survey. HiddenBench (Li et al., ICML 2026) gives groups of 3–4 agents 65 hidden-profile decisions, each agent holding shared and private facts; across 15 LLMs, post-discussion accuracy is 30.1% versus 80.7% for one fully informed agent. The Collaboration Tax (Sun et al., EMNLP 2026) splits 32 generated puzzles (mazes, relational queries, constraint problems) into two private views; averaged over 11 models, pairs lose 28.1% of the solo score, and an LLM judge flags four failure stages per rollout (grounding, querying, integration, re-derivation). Collab-Overcooked (Sun et al., EMNLP 2025) runs 30 recipe tasks in six levels, in which a chef who alone knows the recipe and an assistant work in separate kitchen halves and pass items over a counter; GPT-4o's success falls from 94% to 4% as the required collaboration grows. EscapeBench (Qian et al., ACL 2025) evaluates a single agent on 36 text escape-game settings; the best model makes 29% progress before its first hint, versus 60% for humans.

Proposed method and data. Each EscapeBench room is rebuilt into a pair of mutually invisible rooms by placing every clue and the lock it opens in different rooms; codes and orders can then travel only through dialogue, and keys and tools only through the hatch. The environment logs every observation, message, and action, and aligning these logs with the reference solution locates the stage at which each cross-room clue fails, if it does (non-discovery, non-transmission, or non-uptake). Data are the resulting room pairs, with clue–lock links annotated from the reference solutions.

Proposed evaluation methods and metrics. (i) Success rate: the fraction of episodes in which both exits open within a fixed action budget. (ii) Failure-stage distribution: the share of failed cross-room clues at each stage. Exact-value clues (e.g., digits) are judged by rule, others (e.g., color sequences) by humans; automatic labels are validated on a human-labeled sample. We test LLMs of several sizes.

Proposed timeline. Week 1 (Nov 15–21): environment, logging, and a single-agent pilot on 2–3 easy original rooms (floor-effect check). Week 2 (Nov 22–28): annotation and room rebuilding. Week 3 (Nov 29–Dec 5): Solo and Peer runs across models. Week 4 (Dec 6–12): Oracle runs and label validation. Week 5 (Dec 13–20): analysis and final report.
